Six years of printed gearboxes

Built · on the arm

Inside a printed 1:49

A 72-tooth ring, a 12-tooth sun and three planets give 1:7. Stack a second stage and one NEMA 23 turns into slow torque at the shoulder.

  • Core, housing and input/output are separate parts, so the motor swaps without redesigning the gears.
  • Overloaded to 6 kg on the lever, the motor shaft slipped before a single tooth broke.
  • ABS decides the real number; the 147 N·m is the motor times the ratio.
Built · July 2025

The 2025 arm

Base, shoulder, upper-arm roll and elbow, all through printed planetaries. Three NEMA 17 and one NEMA 23, TB6600 drivers, an ESP32 with a web panel and a joystick.

  • Two configurations: A with an extension link in the upper arm, B with a short link.
  • 681 mm horizontal and 914 mm high at the wrist flange in the long configuration, from the CAD.
  • No wrist yet. Open loop. Lifted its own weight.
Design · simulated, untested

RPV1-B: the base, done properly

The old base had about 0.7 degrees of backlash and a second-stage sun running at six times the stress the material tolerates. The new one is generated by rolling a planet-shaped cutter, the way a gear generator does it, so the profiles are conjugate and backlash is a single parameter tuned with printed coupons.

  • Module 1.5, helix 15 degrees, two identical 14/31/76 stages, steel pins on 6000 bearings, output on two 6010 bearings.
  • Tooth root stress at stalled motor: about 24 MPa by hand, 38 MPa in the finite-element model. The motor skips before the tooth fails.
  • Simulated output backlash about 0.2 degrees. Printed volume about 1.3 litres.
Design · firmware tested in simulation

Reading the output without touching the shaft

The centre of the axis is taken by the motor and the suns, so the output angle comes from a 104-tooth ring on the flange driving a 12-tooth pinion on a 14-bit magnetic encoder: about 142,000 counts per turn of the arm.

  • A Hall switch and a 3 mm magnet give the absolute reference at power-up; a DS18B20 on the motor protects the printed flange.
  • 5,000 Monte Carlo assemblies found the encoder pair would bind in 44 percent of builds. Moving the pinion 0.15 mm and tightening the flange fit brought that under 0.1 percent.
  • ESP32 firmware: homing, closed-loop positioning, backlash measurement, thermal cut-off. Not yet run on hardware.

Nastran from a Python script

The Inventor simulation tools do not take loads and supports from a script reliably, so the Nastran input deck is written by hand: gmsh mesh, loads and supports set in Python, solved by the solver that ships with Inventor. The path was validated on a steel cantilever before touching a gear.

Cantilever check, deflection−0.1905 mm · 0 % error
Sun 2 root, stalled motor38 MPa (Lewis: 24)
D-bore corner, before / after fillet52.6 → 21 MPa
Sun-to-neck junction, before / after60.6 → 39.4 MPa
Carrier ribs, von Mises12.9 MPa · FS 2.3
Safe operating settingdriver current 65 %

Conclusion: with the motor stalled at full current no printed material survives the sun root. The protection is the driver current, not the plastic. At 65 percent the output still gives about 15 N·m, three times what operation needs.

Every pose, mesh against mesh

The 79 part meshes and the four joint axes were exported from Inventor read-only. A Python chain rotates the five links and tests contact between links with python-fcl, plus a check for anything dropping below the table. The 3D model at the top of this page runs on the same exported data.

Within the software limits there is no link-on-link collision in any combination tested. The old base let 33 poses dip under the table; the pedestal removes all of them.

Finding the binding before printing it

The sensor bay was modelled with the real board dimensions, then every tolerance was drawn at random 5,000 times: gear backlash, flange pilot fit, ring eccentricity, magnet position. The first design bound the encoder pair in 44 percent of builds.

Resolution is not the problem: 142,000 counts per turn. The error budget is dominated by how round the printed ring is, which is exactly what the first bench test measures.

Profiles that mesh because they were cut

The old gears came from a spreadsheet with a convention that added clearance to the ring pitch diameter: 0.7 degrees of play at the base. The generator now builds the sun and ring by rolling a planet-shaped cutter through them, so the profiles are conjugate by construction and the 14-tooth sun gets correct root relief.

Python drives Inventor through COM: profiles as polylines, patterns by definition, assembly constraints rebuilt on every regeneration. Change one parameter and the base rebuilds.

Prints flat, machines flat

The old foot was a thin cross around the motor with one bearing taking the whole tipping moment. The pedestal is plates and columns: each part prints flat on a Bambu A1 without supports, and each maps to an aluminium plate or bar with the same holes when the project goes to metal.

Method

Scripted against the CAD

Every part of the 2026 rebuild is generated by Python driving Autodesk Inventor, so when a bench test changes a parameter the whole base regenerates. The order below is the order things actually happen in.

Diagnose

Measure the old part. Backlash, stress at stall, which bearing takes the tipping moment.

Generate

Involute profiles by rolling a cutter; the 2D meshing check before any 3D.

Simulate

Nastran from a hand-written deck, gmsh mesh, Monte Carlo on tolerances, magnetic field at the sensors.

Print

Flat on a Bambu A1, no supports. Each plate maps to an aluminium part with the same holes.

Measure

Eight bench phases with a pass criterion each. Results replace the simulated numbers as they come.

Simulation is where I look. The bench is where I believe.

Field notes

What broke

The 2026 rebuild starts from these. Each one has a number because each one was measured or computed after the fact.

Ring gear backlash

A spreadsheet convention added clearance to the ring pitch diameter. The base had about 0.7 degrees of play, roughly 8 mm at the end of the long arm.

0.7° · 8 mm at the tip

Sun gear at stall

Tooth root stress on the second-stage sun with the motor stalled came out around 152 MPa, for a material that tolerates about 25. The tooth would break before the motor skipped.

152 MPa vs 25 MPa allowed

One bearing, all the tipping

A single 6816 held the whole arm against a 17.5 N·m tipping moment with the arm stretched out. Carriers cracked and delaminated around it.

17.5 N·m on one 6816
